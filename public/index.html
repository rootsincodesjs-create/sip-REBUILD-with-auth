Hello and welcome to my web page. My name is Sarah Jaimes Santos — a first-generation Mexican American college student, digital creator, and advocate for equity in STEM. As an indigenous woman in tech, I’ve often noticed the lack of faces like mine within my STEM spaces. This project is my way of changing that. Our stories in STEM are powerful and long overdue to be seen. I created this space to shine a light on the vital yet often overlooked contributions of women of color in science, technology, engineering, and mathematics. More than just a digital archive, this platform is a growing community — a place where our stories are celebrated, our voices amplified, and our journeys honored. My goal is to empower women of color, past and present, by recognizing their achievements, acknowledging their struggles, and building meaningful connections across generations.

Discrimination Against Women of Color in STEM

Data, Lived Inequalities, and Systemic Structures

Overview

Despite decades of diversity initiatives, STEM in the United States remain dominated by whiteness and maleness. Women of color experience compounded forms of discrimination shaped by race, gender, class, and power heirarchies. these inequities are not the result of individual bias alone, but of systemic structures that determine who is seen as competent, who is finded, who advances, and who is pushed out.

This section presents key statistics documenting discrimination against women of color in STEM and outlines the institutional mechanisms that continue to reproduce exclusion.

Representation in STEM

💠In the United States, women are significantly less likely than men to work in STEM occupations. As of 2021, only 18% of employed women worked in STEM fields compared to 30% of employed men (National Science Foundation).

💠Women of color remain particularly underrepresented, especially in high-paying and leadership-oriented STEM fields such as engineering, computer science, and physics.

💠Representation declines further at higher academic and professional ranks, a phenomenon often referred to as the “leaky pipeline.”

Workplace Discrimination in STEM

Gender Discrimination

According to a national survey by the Pew Research Center:

    💠50% of women working in STEM report experiencing gender-based discrimination at work.

    💠Common forms include:

    • Being paid less than a man doing the same job (29%)
    • Being treated as if they were not competent (29%)
    • Experiencing repeated small slights or microaggressions (20%)
    • Receiving less support from senior leadership (18%)

    💠In majority-male STEM workplaces, 78% of women report experiencing gender discrimination.

    Race and Ethnicity Discrimination

    💠62% of Black STEM workers report experiencing race-based discrimination at work.

    💠42% of Hispanic STEM workers report experiencing race-based discrimination at work.

    💠These experiences include being passed over for opportunities, having expertise questioned, and facing exclusion from professional networks.

    For women of color, gendered and racial discrimination often occur simultaneously and cannot be separated.

Sexual Harassment in Academic STEM Enviornments

A comprehensive report by the National Academics of Sciences, Engineering, and Medicine (NASEM) found:

    💠Approximately 20% of female science students,

    💠Over 20% of female science engineering students and,

    💠More than 40% of female medical students reported experiencing Sexual harassment from falculty or staff

The report emphasizes that harassment functions as a systemic sorting mechanism, driving women---especially women of color---out of STEM fields by creating hostile and unsafe enviornments.

Pay Inequality and Economic Disparities

Across the U.S. workforce:

    💠Latina and Native American women earn approximately 58 cents for every dollar earned by white, non-Hispanic men.

    💠Black women earn approximately 66 cents per dollar

These wage gaps shape who can afford to remain in STEM careers, pursue advanced degrees, or take unpaid research and leadership opportunities.

Funding Inequities and Research Valuation

Structural bias also appears in research funding:

    💠Studies of National Institutes of Health (NIH) funding show that research topic accounts for more than 20% of the funding gap between Black and White principal investigators.

    💠Topics more frequently proposed by Black scientists--often community-focused or equity-oriented--recieve lower funding rates, reinforcing, whose knowledge is deemed "fundamental" or "legitimate"

Bias in Evaluation and "Merit"

Experimental studies demonstrate that:

    💠Identical applications are rated as more competent and hireable when associated with a male name rather than a female name

    💠Male-named applicants are offered higher salaries and more mentoring, even when qualifications are identical

These findings challenges the idea that STEM operates as a purely meritocratic space.

Systemic Structures That Reinforce Whiteness and Maleness in STEM

The dominance of white men in STEM is sustained by interconnected systems:

    💠Biased definitions of merit that privilege confidence, pedigree, and elite networks.

    💠Hierarchical power structures, where advancement depends on advisors, supervisors, or principle investigators who are overwhelmingly white and male.

    💠Harassment and hostile climates that function as barriers to retention.

    💠Intersectional penalties, where women of color face both racialized and gendered stereotypes.

    💠Funding and topic hierarchies that devalue research centered on marginalized communities.

    💠Cumulative disadvantage, where small inequities in pay, mentorship, and recognition compound over time.

Why This Matters

These patterns are not accidental. They reflect how STEM institutions have been historically structured and how power continues to be distributed. Addressing inequity in STEM requires not only increasing representation, but also transforming the systems that define legitimacy, success, and belonging,

Resources & Solidarity

Being a woman of color in STEM shouldn’t mean having to be perfect every second just to earn a place. Yet too often, the expectation is that we prove ourselves again and again in male-dominated spaces. This constant pressure can feel isolating, but it also shows why building community matters.

By uplifting each other’s voices and normalizing our presence in these fields, we shift the narrative away from survival towards belonging. Women of color shouldn’t have to fight for our spot—we should already be seen as an integral part of STEM. Community allows us to create that reality together: where our successes are celebrated, our challenges are shared, and our contributions are recognized without question. Therefore, helping indigenous communities should be our top priority. We are not free, until we are all free
